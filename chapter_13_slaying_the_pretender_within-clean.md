# Chapter 13: Slaying the Pretender Within

We read in the scriptures, especially in the Tenth Canto of *Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam*, that one day, Lord Kṛṣṇa and Lord Balarāma entered the forest of Vṛndāvana to graze the cows along with Their cowherd boyfriends [1]. 

Every single day, the cowherd boys enter the forest with their hearts bursting with joy, eager to play and serve the Lord. But on this particular day, an intruder was waiting to test their sincerity from within their own midst. 

---

## **The Transformation of Summer**

When Kṛṣṇa, Balarāma, and the cowherd boys stepped into the forest, it was the height of the summer season. Normally, the Indian summer is incredibly harsh, parching, and exhausting. The rays of the sun beat down like poison, parching the earth, drying up the green grass, and driving everyone to seek shelter. But because the Supreme Personality of Godhead was personally walking on the ground, the forest underwent a supernatural, ecstatic change [1]. 

Summer instantly manifested the sweet, cool, and pleasing qualities of spring! [1] 

Loud, cascading waterfalls drowned out the noisy crickets with their deep vibrations. Clusters of trees were constantly moistened by the fine, sweet spray of those waterfalls, keeping the entire forest lush, damp, and cool. The wind, wafting over the gentle waves of the lakes, carried the cooling, fragrant pollen of golden lotuses and water lilies across the pasture lands [1]. Even the fierce summer heat and the threat of forest fires could not touch the residents of Vṛndāvana. The deep rivers drenched their banks with flowing waves, keeping the clay moist and muddy, so the parching sun could not evaporate the earth's vital sap [1].

---

## **Mother Yaśodā’s Anxious Love**

Before the boys entered the forest, Mother Yaśodā was feeling the deep pain of maternal anxiety (*vātsalya-rasa*) [2]. 

She sat at home, her heart trembling with affection, and one day told Kṛṣṇa and Balarāma: *“I want to go with you to the forest.”*

Kṛṣṇa did not like this idea. He frowned His face [2]. He wanted to play freely without any rules or parental control. 

Yaśodā tried to persuade Him, saying: *“Do not worry about the household duties. Rohiṇī Devī will stay back and manage the home today, and tomorrow she will go and I will stay back. In this way, we can share the service of protecting you.”* [2]

But Kṛṣṇa remained silent. In spiritual etiquette, Kṛṣṇa did not speak because His senior brother, Balarāma, was present [2]. He submissively allowed Balarāma to speak for Him. 

Balarāma stepped forward, smiled warmly, and spoke the truth directly: *“My dear Mother, let me tell you the actual truth. We boys are not going to the forest to tend the cows. The cows naturally take care of themselves. We only go there to play! If you come with us, you will completely interrupt our enjoyment! You will say: ‘No, don't climb that tree! Don't run there, you will fall down! Don't touch that!’ You will control us, and we will not be able to play freely. If you really want to come with us, you must become a gopa boy first! Otherwise, please go back to the house.”* [2]

Hearing Balarāma speak His inner mind so perfectly, Kṛṣṇa smiled. Mother Yaśodā submissively accepted Balarāma’s words and returned home [2].

---

## **The Ornaments of Vṛndāvana’s Forest**

The moment the boys were out of their mothers’ sight, they removed all the heavy, expensive gold ornaments and jewels that had been placed on their bodies [3]. 

They threw them aside and decorated themselves with the natural, simple wealth of Vṛndāvana [3]:
- They wore newly grown leaves and peacock feathers.
- They hung garlands of fragrant Mallikā (jasmine) flowers around their necks [3].
- They put delicate Śirīṣa flowers in their ears as earrings [3].
- They decorated their hair with colored minerals and beautiful Kruci flowers [3].

Dressed in these simple forest clothes, Kṛṣṇa, Balarāma, and the cowherd boys danced, wrestled, and sang. Sometimes Kṛṣṇa would dance, and the boys would play the flute, hand cymbals, and buffalo horns to accompany Him. At other times, Balarāma and the boys would dance, and Kṛṣṇa would loudly praise them: *“Very good! Wonderful!”* [3] Unseen by the boys, demigods disguised themselves as members of the cowherd community and joined that gathering too, worshipping Kṛṣṇa and Balarāma the way one dancer praises another [3]—the very same disguise Pralambāsura would soon put on for the opposite reason.

---

## **The Innocent Games of Vṛndāvana**

The pasture grounds of Vṛndāvana were a limitless playground. The boys whirled about until they became dizzy (*laghanai*), leaped like frogs, chased one another, built bridges, and played with bilva or kumbha fruits [3]. 

They played the game of *netra-bandha* (blindfolding), where one boy would approach a blindfolded boy from behind and place his palms over his eyes [3]. Simply by the feel of the palms, the blindfolded boy had to guess who his friend was. If he won, he would claim a walking stick or a flute as a prize [3].

At midday, when Kṛṣṇa lay resting, the young gopīs would pass by singing, and His boyfriends would pretend to innocently ask them the price of milk—only to steal their yogurt and run off laughing [3].

Sometimes, they would play at the riverbank of the Yamunā, acting like customs officers. They would block the path of the young gopīs and jokingly demand: *“This forest belongs to our King! Before you cross the river, you must pay a heavy customs tax on your milk and yogurt!”* The forest was filled with innocent joking, laughter, and spiritual happiness [3].

Every day, the boys wrestled and fought playfully like this—but this was never the fighting of a Pralambāsura. Their fighting existed only to give Kṛṣṇa pleasure; the fighting of a demon or a sinful person exists only to destroy Kṛṣṇa. The two can look identical from the outside. What separates them is entirely what is happening on the inside.

---

## **The Disguise of Duplicity**

While the boys were in the middle of their play, a powerful demon named Pralambāsura entered their midst [4]. He came with a very specific, dark intention: to kidnap Kṛṣṇa and Balarāma [4]. 

But Pralambāsura was a coward. He did not attack openly. Instead, he disguised himself as a cowherd boy, specifically mimicking Madhumāṅgala who had stayed home that day with duties to perform, and joined the playing party [4]. 

In our Gauḍīya Vaiṣṇava theology, Pralambāsura represents **deceit, crookedness, and duplicity (*kapaṭatā*)**. He represents the dangerous tendency to pretend to be a devotee on the outside while internally harboring completely separate, self-serving, and poisonous motivations [4].

Let us look at the history of ancient India in the *Mahābhārata*. The whole world knows that Duryodhana learned the art of club-fighting from Lord Balarāma Himself. But Duryodhana's heart was never simple or sincere—tradition describes him as carrying within him the very spirit of Kali, the personified demon of discord and deceit [9]. Even as Balarāma trained him faithfully, Duryodhana's own crookedness eventually caught up with him: when Bhīmasena struck him an unlawful blow on the battlefield, breaking the very rules of the art Balarāma had taught him, Balarāma became furious and nearly slew Bhīmasena on the spot—until Kṛṣṇa Himself calmed His brother down by reminding Him of Duryodhana's own long history of adharma [9]. In the end, crookedness always meets its own reckoning.

There was only one Pralambāsura in Vṛndāvana five thousand years ago. But today, how many Pralambāsuras do we have? There are so many Pralambāsuras in our movement! They walk among us, attend programs, and wear the Vaiṣṇava dress, but their hearts are completely full of politics, business, and deceit. 

---

## **The Game of Carriers**

Lord Kṛṣṇa, who sees everything, instantly understood the demon's mind [5]. But He did not expose him immediately. He pretended to accept him as a friend and proposed a game of teams [5]: *“Hey, boys! Let's play now. We will divide ourselves into two even teams, with Me leading one team and Balarāma leading the other.”* [5]

The winners of the game would climb onto the backs of the losers, who would have to carry them to the banyan tree known as Bhāṇḍīraka [5]. 

Kṛṣṇa cleverly arranged the game so that His own team would lose [5]. Why did He do this? 

Pralambāsura had joined Kṛṣṇa’s team because he was thinking: *“Kṛṣṇa is too powerful to attack directly. I don't want to go directly to Him. But Balarāma is close to Him. If I carry Balarāma, I can take Him far away and kill Him. Once Balarāma is dead, Kṛṣṇa will die of grief on His own.”* [5]

Kṛṣṇa knew this deceitful plan perfectly. He willingly let His team lose so the demon would be forced to carry Balarāma [5]. Defeated in the game, Kṛṣṇa carried Śrīdāmā on His back, Bhadrasena carried Vṛṣabha, and Pralambāsura carried Balarāma, the son of Rohiṇī [5].

---

## **The Weight of the Supreme Lord**

Pralambāsura carried Balarāma on his shoulders, but instead of putting Him down at the Bhāṇḍīraka banyan tree, he kept running [6]. He ran swiftly, carrying Balarāma far beyond the playground [6].

As the demon ran, Lord Balarāma suddenly made Himself **as heavy as massive Mount Sumeru!** [6] 

The immense weight crushed the demon's shoulders. In panic, Pralambāsura was forced to abandon his disguise. He assumed his actual, gigantic demoniac form—an effulgent body covered in golden ornaments that resembled a dark cloud flashing with lightning [6]. 

His body touched the sky. He had blazing eyes, fiery hair, terrible teeth, and reached up to his scowling, angry brows [7]. 

When Lord Balarāma saw this massive demon carrying Him swiftly through the sky, He acted as if He were a little frightened and bewildered [7]. In Kavi Karṇapūra’s *Ānanda-vṛndāvana-campu*, it is described that Balarāma called out to His brother: *“Dāmodara! Manoram! Where are you? This giant demon is taking Me away in a different direction! Please help Me!”* [10]

But Kṛṣṇa called back: *“Don't call Me! Don't you know who You are? You are the source of all strength! Deal with him Yourself! This is a chance to show Your power!”* [7]

---

## **Slaying the Pretender**

Hearing Kṛṣṇa’s words, Lord Balarāma became furious [8]. 

He did not even take up His club or His plow. He simply clenched His hand and **hammered a single, crushing blow with His fist directly onto the demon's head!** [8] Lord Balarāma's hand possesses the strength of billions of thunderbolts.

Just as Indra strikes a mountain with his thunderbolt, Balarāma’s fist cracked the demon’s skull open [8]. Pralambāsura immediately vomited blood, lost all consciousness, and fell lifeless to the ground with a massive crash, like a mountain devastated by Indra [8]. 

The cowherd boys ran forward, completely astonished, embraced Balarāma with ecstatic love, and threw their arms around Him as if He had come back from the dead, crying: *“Excellent! Well done! All glories to Balarāma!”* The demigods in the heavens showered beautiful flower garlands and chanted His glories [8].

Balarāma’s plow is a gardening tool. It is meant to till the hard, dry ground of our hearts, tilling the soil so the seed of devotion (*bhakti-latā-bīja*) can be planted and safely grow. But His fist is a thunderbolt meant to crush our duplicity. 

When we celebrate Balarāma Jayantī, we spend so much time on external functions—cleaning, cooking, decorating, and waiting to break our fast. We can hire people to clean, we can hire people to decorate, and we can even hire people to cook. 

But let me tell you straight: **you cannot hire anyone to do your hearing and praying!** That is your personal business with Balarāma. The primary purpose of Balarāma's appearance is to kill the demons, and on this special day, He is coming to kill the demon of **deceit and duplicity** in our lives.

Let us pray honestly to Lord Balarāma to help us remove the Pralambāsura of deceit in our lives.

Take a few honest, quiet minutes before Lord Balarāma's plow to examine your consciousness:

1.  **My Disguise:** Where am I carrying separate, selfish, or material desires (for fame, respect, or control) while outwardly presenting myself as a dedicated, humble devotee? `_________________________`

3.  **Cheating the Guru:** Am I pretending to strictly follow the instructions of my spiritual master while secretly compromising my vows and standards when no one is watching? `_________________________`
4.  **The Fast vs. The Heart:** Am I more focused on the external, cosmetic rituals of my devotional life (decorations, fasting times, feasting) than on the internal, painful work of begging for purification? `_________________________`

*   **The Vow of Honesty:** Settle this standard in your mind: *“Lord Balarāma cannot be cheated. If I try to cheat Him, I will only cheat myself of His mercy. I will lay down my disguise and speak the raw, unpolished truth to my spiritual master today.”*
