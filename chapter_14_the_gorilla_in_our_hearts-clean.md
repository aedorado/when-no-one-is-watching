# Chapter 14: The Gorilla in Our Hearts

In our Gauḍīya Vaiṣṇava tradition, Lord Balarāma is celebrated as the protector who slays the specific obstacles in our hearts. Having conquered duplicity in Pralambāsura, we must now turn our attention to the raging gorilla of our animalistic anger, pride of strength, and offenses toward devotees—the pastime of Dvivida Gorilla [1].

---

## **The Danger of Material Opulence**

Dvivida was not an ordinary ape. He was an extremely powerful, celebrated, and heroic associate of Lord Rāmacandra who had fought valiantly in the Battle of Lanka [1]. He had put his entire heart and strength into serving Sugrīva, Hanumān, and Lord Rāmacandra, bringing immense pleasure to the Lord with his fighting. When the war ended, Lord Rāmacandra sent His victorious associates back to Ayodhyā and beyond. But Dvivida, along with Mahendra and Jāmbavān, did not go—not because of any offense at that time, but because the Lord had further service reserved for them. A great destiny was waiting for Dvivida.

But after Lord Rāmacandra departed from this world, Dvivida's heart became contaminated. He became intensely proud of his immense physical strength (*bala-garva*) [2]. 

Because of this pride, he committed a massive, fatal offense: he disrespected Lord Rāmacandra's chaste brother, Lakṣmaṇa, and other saintly sages [2]. In his arrogance, he looked at Lakṣmaṇa and said: *“You are only the brother of the Supreme Lord, you are not Supreme like the Lord Himself! I am more powerful than you!”* This offense created a dark cloud of bad fortune around him, and he fell into bad association, becoming close friends with the demon Narakāsura [2]. Śrīla Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura confirms that Dvivida's ruin was the direct result of this offense to great personalities—the Lord Himself arranged the fall, to show the world the danger of bad association that comes from disrespecting exalted souls [2].

This teaches us an extremely heavy lesson: **opulence (*aiśvarya*) is safe in the spiritual world because there are no side effects, but in this material world, it is a dangerous poison.** The moment you get high intelligence, wealthy resources, or managerial power, the Dvivida Gorilla of pride enters your heart [2]. You begin to think: *“I am so smart. I am so successful. Who are these other devotees? I don't need to respect them.”* At that very moment, your spiritual roots are cut, and you fall down [2].

This is exactly how offenses work: like food particles left uncleaned between the teeth, they accumulate quietly, day after day, until they harden into something your own teeth can no longer remove—something that now needs a far more painful extraction. It rarely begins with the Guru or with Kṛṣṇa Himself. It begins small, with a minor disrespect toward a fellow devotee. Left unaddressed, that one offense matures, and grows, and reaches further—until it finally arrives at the Guru, and then at Kṛṣṇa. Dvivida's disrespect to Lakṣmaṇa was exactly this kind of small beginning.

This is why, when Śrīla Narottama dāsa Ṭhākura was sent by his spiritual master, Lokanātha Gosvāmī, to Navadvīpa, Lokanātha Gosvāmī's instruction to him was simple and direct: *"Do not offend any Vaiṣṇava."* [10] That single warning was the real training a disciple needs—far more than any technical knowledge of scripture.

---

## **The Havoc of Demoniac Anger**

When Narakāsura was killed by Lord Kṛṣṇa, Dvivida was consumed by blinding, destructive, and vindictive anger [2]. He wanted to avenge his friend’s death, so he began to devastate kingdoms and cities, especially the province of Ānarta where Kṛṣṇa lived [2].

His acts of destruction were terrifying [2]:
- He set fire to villages, cities, mining places, and cowherd residential quarters where devotees were busy tilling fields and protecting cows [2].
- He entered the ocean and, with the strength of ten thousand elephants, churned the water with his massive arms—which were kilometers wide—and submerged the surrounding coastal regions [2]. He also tore up entire mountains and hurled them to devastate the neighboring kingdoms [2].
- He tore down the trees in the hermitages of exalted sages and contaminated their sacred sacrificial fires with his feces and urine [3].
- He arrogantly captured men and women, locked them inside mountain caves, and sealed the cave entrances with massive chunks of stone and boulders, leaving them to die without food or water [3].

We must ask ourselves: **Do we treat people like this sometimes?** When someone is in need, we deny them. We ignore people, saying: *“You are not important. You are not on my level. Get away.”* When we ignore people, shut them out, or give them the silent treatment, we are locking them inside the cave of our coldness, leaving them to starve! This is Dvivida Gorilla behavior. 

This is the nature of anger: it is completely blind, senseless, and destructive. Śrīmatī Rādhārāṇī also gets angry with Kṛṣṇa, but her anger is pure love—she never destroys cities or causes anxiety to any devotee. But demoniac anger destroys everything.

---

## **The Mocking of Raivataka Mountain**

One day, Lord Balarāma was enjoying Himself on the beautiful Raivataka Mountain [4]. He was adorned with a gorgeous lotus garland, appearing most attractive in every limb, and singing amidst a crowd of young women [4]. His body shone brilliantly, His complexion was as white as an autumn cloud, He wore blue garments, and His eyes rolled in ecstasy from drinking Vāruṇī liquor [4]. He appeared like an elephant in rut.

This is the very opulence that is safe to behold: in the *Nectar of Devotion*, one devotee describes Balarāma's own transcendental beauty exactly this way—*"Let me take shelter of the lotus feet of Balarāma, whose beauty is enhanced by His earrings touching His cheeks. His face is decorated with tilaka made of kastūrī [musk], and His broad chest is decorated with a garland of guñjā. His complexion is as white as an autumn cloud, He wears garments of blue, and His voice is very grave. His arms are very long, touching His thighs, and He has shown His great strength by killing the demon Pralamba. Let me take shelter of this chivalrous Balarāma, who is always victorious."* [9] Opulence like this has no side effects, because it exists only in service to the Lord Himself.

A demon cannot bear to see others happy—it is the very opposite of a devotee, who naturally rejoices at another's joy. So the mischievous ape, Dvivida, climbed a nearby tree branch and began to shake the trees violently, making loud, mocking "Kila, kila" sounds [4]. 

When Lord Balarāma’s consorts saw the ape's impudence, they began to laugh [5]. This only fueled Dvivida's arrogance. 

He came right in front of them, made obscene gestures with his eyebrows, bared his teeth, and turned around to show them his anus! [5] Śrīla Viśvanātha Cakravartī Ṭhākura writes that Dvivida would come right up to the women, move about, and urinate right in front of them [5]. He was incredibly crude and offensive.

Dvivida then grabbed Lord Balarāma's jar of Vāruṇī liquor, broke it in His face, laughed mockingly, and began pulling at the clothing of the young girls! [6] This was a direct, blasphemous offense against the Supreme Lord and His intimate associates [6]. Balarāma's own drinking was transcendental play, utterly harmless. But the real intoxicant here was Dvivida's own pride of strength—and that pride intoxicates far more dangerously than any liquor ever could.

---

## **The Battle of Strength**

Seeing this ape's insolence, Lord Balarāma also remembered everything he had done before this—the burned villages, the flooded coastlines, the innocent men and women left to starve in sealed caves. Furious, He took up His club, Sunandaka, and His plow to put this enemy to death [6]. 

Dvivida also rushed forward to battle. With one hand, he uprooted a massive sal tree, ran toward Balarāma, and struck Him directly on the head with the tree trunk! [7] 

But Lord Sankarṣaṇa remained as motionless as a mountain [7]. He simply caught the log as it fell upon His head, and struck Dvivida with His club, Sunandaka [7]. 

Struck on the skull, Dvivida began to bleed profusely, looking like a mountain beautified by red oxide [7]. Ignoring the wound, he uprooted another tree, stripped it of leaves with his bare fingers, and struck Balarāma again [7]. Balarāma instantly shattered the tree into a hundred pieces [7]. Dvivida grabbed another tree, and another, but Balarāma smashed them all [7].

The forest was laid bare and treeless [8]. Dvivida then began throwing massive boulders, but Balarāma’s club powdered them into dust [8]. 

Here we must understand a deep mystery: **why were the innocent trees killed?** What did they do? What sin did they commit? They were completely helpless. Śrīla Prabhupāda says there are two answers: 1) they were suffering from their own past karma, and 2) they were touched by the transcendental body of Lord Balarāma during His pastime, and through that touch, they were instantly liberated and returned back to the spiritual world [8]! Even the Lord's collateral damage is supreme mercy.

---

## **Crushing the Gorilla Within**

Finally, having no more weapons left, the powerful ape clenched his fists and rushed bare-handed toward Balarāma [8]. 

Lord Balarāma calmly threw aside His club and plow, and with His bare hands, **delivered a crushing hammer blow directly to Dvivida's collarbone!** [8]

The gorilla’s collarbone shattered [8]. Dvivida vomited blood, fell lifeless to the ground, and shook the entire Raivataka Mountain as he fell, like a ship tossed in a raging sea [8]. The demigods in the heavens danced, crying: *“Victory to Rāma! Excellent! Well done!”* and showered flowers upon Him [8].

We must ask ourselves honestly: **What is the strength of our anger? Is it beneficial or destructive?** 

If our behavior, our sharp words, or our management style causes anxiety and pain to other devotees, it means the Dvivida Gorilla is sitting active inside our own hearts! If we do not pray to Lord Balarāma to destroy this beast, our temple festivals and Sādhana are just a cheap exercise in eating prasādam. 

Let us pray sincerely from the very ceiling of our hearts: *“Dauji! The Dvivida Gorilla of anger and pride is roaring inside my heart, destroying the forest of my Bhakti. I cannot control this beast—it has the strength of ten thousand elephants. Please, take Your bare hands and strike my heart. Crush this inner beast, drag it out of my soul, and let me remain a simple, submissive servant of Your devotees.”*

Take a few quiet, honest minutes before Lord Balarāma's club to examine your consciousness:

1.  **My Anger:** When things do not go my way, do I fly into a rage, setting verbal fires that burn down the peace and devotion of those around me? `_________________________`
2.  **My Pride of Strength (*Bala-Garva*):** Am I proud of my intelligence, my preaching results, or my seniority, using it to look down on newer or less-empowered devotees? `_________________________`
3.  **My Insolence:** Have I ever mocked, disrespected, or spoken offensively about senior Vaiṣṇavas or spiritual authorities because of my own stubborn opinions? `_________________________`
4.  **The Collateral Damage:** Have my angry, prideful outbursts hurt innocent, helpless people around me (my spouse, my children, my disciples)? `_________________________`

*   **The Prayer of the Helpless:** Next time you feel the hot current of anger rising in your chest, stop, close your eyes, and scream internally: *“Balarāma! The gorilla is loose! Please, strike him down before I commit an offense!”*
