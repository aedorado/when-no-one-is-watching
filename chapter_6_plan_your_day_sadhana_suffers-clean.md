# Chapter 6: An Unfortunate Jaḍa Bharata

There is a setting in the fifth canto of the *Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam* (5.11.8), Chapter Eleven, where the great self-realized soul Jaḍa Bharata is instructing King Rahūgaṇa. 

The text states:
> *“When the living entity’s mind becomes absorbed in the sense gratification of the material world, it brings about its conditioned life and suffering within a material situation. However, when the mind becomes unattached to material enjoyment, it becomes the cause of liberation.”*[1]

It is therefore concluded that the mind is the cause of material existence and liberation also. Everyone is suffering in this material world because of the mind. It is the mind that causes all the sufferings we are going through. Our present situation and body were created by our past minds, and our next body is being created by our mind right now. If we keep our minds engaged in material activities, it will cause continuous bondage and make us remain in this world in different bodies, suffering the consequences of our actions. Therefore, the only real solution is to engage the mind fully in Kṛṣṇa conscious activity. This is called spiritual engagement, as Kṛṣṇa Himself confirms: *“One who engages in full devotional service, who does not fall down in any circumstance, at once transcends the modes of material nature and thus comes to the level of Brahman.”*[2]

To explain this properly, our scriptures give a beautiful analogy of a lamp. When the flame in the lamp burns the wick improperly, the lamp becomes blackened with soot. But when the lamp is filled with pure ghee and is burning properly, it gives bright illumination. 

Improper burning is the misuse of our mind—when the mind is absorbed in material sense gratification, it blackens our consciousness and causes suffering. But a lamp filled with ghee is when a person’s mind is properly engaged in Kṛṣṇa consciousness. When the mind is detached from material enjoyment and filled with the ghee of devotion, it brings about the original brightness and enlightenment of the soul. 

So if anyone wants to be happy in this world, we have to adjust the mind to a better situation. But how? Just as we approach a professional to gain knowledge in any other field, if we want to know God, we have to approach a professional in that field too—someone who already knows the destination because he has already arrived there.

---

## **The Fortunate Jaḍa Bharata**

To understand how to train the mind, let us look at the history of Jaḍa Bharata, a professional in exactly this sense, and the conversation he had with King Rahūgaṇa. There are two worlds—the material world and the spiritual world—and they are not the same. They are a completely different experience altogether.

Before his father, Ṛṣabhadeva, resigned as emperor, he called his children together and told them: *“You want to be happy? Purify your heart. You want to purify your heart? Perform austerity.”*[4] There are two types of austerity—one for sense gratification, and one to please Kṛṣṇa. He wanted his sons to choose the austerity that pleases Kṛṣṇa.

Jaḍa Bharata was once a great emperor who renounced his entire kingdom, opulence, fame, and power to go to the forest to practice devotion. But while in the forest, he became distracted by his attachment to a baby deer. He forgot what took him to the forest, and eventually died thinking of the deer.

But let me tell you: Jaḍa Bharata was a *fortunate* Jaḍa Bharata. Why? Because he made his mistake only once, and he learned his lesson—as the saying goes, once bitten, twice shy. For him, it was not even a mistake at all—not a normal case, but an extreme case, a pastime he was playing. Kṛṣṇa used him to teach all of us a lesson so that we would be careful while dealing with the material energy. In his next life, Jaḍa Bharata planned his Kṛṣṇa consciousness perfectly. He blocked himself completely from the world, pretending to be deaf and dumb so that nothing could enter his mind. He was living in this world, but he was not of this world. 

But many of us are *unfortunate* Jaḍa Bharatas. Some of us left our family, left our secular education, and abandoned everything to come to Kṛṣṇa consciousness. But after some years, we drift back to the very things we rejected. 

In Sanskrit, they have a word for this: *vāntāśī*. *Vāntāśī* means one who eats his own vomit. To leave material sense gratification behind, only to gradually take it back again, is a condemned, unfortunate life. We make mistakes a hundred times and still do not learn, whereas the fortunate Jaḍa Bharata learned everything from a single mistake.

## **The Mind’s False Promise**

Everyone thinks: if I get this one thing, I will be happy. If I get that new device, that's it—I will need nothing else, I will be the best person in the world. But what about the company that made it? How happy are they? Happy, or frustrated?

The mind always tricks us. It says: *“You are not happy? That is because you don’t have this object. When you get it, you will be happy.”* The mind will never tell us that this is not the way. 

Let me tell you about a certain wealthy industrialist. At that time, he was among the richest men in the world. He had only one child, a son who was studying animal husbandry. When the son finished his studies and was preparing his dissertation, he went into the forest to study animal life—and because he went in without security, an animal made a feast out of him. He was gone! 

Journalists went to this man and said, *“You are the wealthiest man in the country, you must be the happiest person in the world.”* 

But he replied, *“I am not the happiest person. I am the most unhappy person in the world. My only child died. All my wealth, my sacrifices, everything is a waste. How can I be happy?”* 

But look at the foolishness of the mind. Those who ride in comfort on what his companies built, sipping a drink and looking out the window, think, *“See how I am enjoying! I wish I were him.”* The man himself was miserable, but we are dreaming of being him!

In July 2025, and again in July 2026, I went to Purī for the Ratha Yātrā festival. That second time, when Jagannātha was returning to His throne, thousands of us stood there in the rain, crying, *“Jai Jagannātha!”* But look at what many of us were really asking for in our hearts. Not shelter. Not devotion. Just, *“Please make me as wealthy as him!”* We do so much austerity just for some material adjustment, because the mind has convinced us that if we get what we want, we will be happy. 

We must train our minds to be detached from these false promises.

Kṛṣṇa Himself tells us why this training matters so much. He says that for one whose mind is untrained in Kṛṣṇa consciousness, the mind is the greatest enemy—but for one whose mind is trained, the mind becomes the best of friends.[3] It is the same mind either way. Untrained, it will keep inventing new figures like this for us to envy. Trained, it becomes absorbed in Kṛṣṇa consciousness, and the moment it is absorbed, the Supersoul is already realized, and we are already on our way back home, back to Godhead.

## **Squeezing the Dead Body**

Because Jaḍa Bharata had planned his consciousness perfectly, he was completely carefree and situated in pure devotion. 

One day, Mahārāja Rahūgaṇa forced Jaḍa Bharata to carry his palanquin. Because Jaḍa Bharata was walking carefully to avoid stepping on ants, the palanquin was jerking. The king became angry and shouted: *“What is this? Are you a dead body? Don’t you know you are carrying a king?”*

Jaḍa Bharata quietly looked at him and said: *“Today you are a king, and I am carrying you. Tomorrow, you will be my carrier, and I will be the king. You want to punish me back into obedience—but that is like trying to grind iron powder back into iron. Once it is ground, it only goes one way. The more you grind it, the more it disappears; it will never return to what it was. You cannot punish me back into a servant, because that person is already gone.”*[5]

When the king heard these words, he offered his obeisances, accepted initiation from him, and was delivered.

Jaḍa Bharata was teaching us a heavy lesson: this material body is just a dead body. And when our mind is focusing on gratifying the dead body, is that life? 

No! But the mind convinces us: *“Squeeze it! Squeeze the dead body! You are not doing it properly, squeeze it more, squeeze it more, and you will get some juice from it!”* But let me ask you: how can juice come out of a dead body? Squeeze it all you want, but you will get nothing. 

An intelligent person, before chasing something, checks: has anyone who got there before me actually been happy? So who has gotten what we're all chasing, before us? Someone might say: Lord Brahmā. His life is three hundred eleven billion years—if anyone has had the time to enjoy, surely it is him. But was Brahmā happy? Was he even safe? One day, powerful demons—Madhu and Kaitabha—appeared near him, and Brahmā himself ran away in fear, because he had not created them; they existed even before him, and he did not know what they were. If Brahmā, with all that longevity, still had to run in fear, is that happiness? But the mind does not allow us to check any of this. It tells us: *“Don't worry about those who failed. They were fools. Only you are intelligent. Go ahead.”*

The mind tells us: *“The better you gratify your senses, the better you will enjoy.”* But that is a lie. The more the senses are gratified, the worse our spiritual situation becomes. Yet we keep trying, because our mind is not trained. If we want to be happy, we must practice detachment and remove the mind from material enjoyment.

## **Early Morning Sādhana as Our Protective Shield**

How do we practically train our minds and protect our consciousness while living in this world? We have to plan our day.

You have to do your job to maintain your body, but you should never do it at the expense of your spiritual life. The two of them must go on, and we must not neglect either. The secret is to get up early, do your job, and then you are free.

I did a five-day seminar on the importance of Brahma Muhūrta. If you can chant your sixteen rounds before 6:30 AM, you will have a very long, wonderful day. "Long day" means you have time to do more things—more reading, more hearing, and more services. 

But if you don’t chant in the morning, you don’t have a day. It becomes a passionate struggle where nothing goes right. Everything is done with passion, there is no goodness, and your entire day is just a struggle.

Chanting your rounds in the morning is like taking a **vaccine** or a **jab** before you step out of your house. Once you have taken your morning vaccine of sādhana and prasāda, you can enter your office and walk among non-devotees, and no material disease or contamination will enter your heart. Morning sādhana is our only strength. It is how we can face the day very nicely. If you do not plan your morning, your entire day will suffer.

---

## **Establishing Direct Boundaries to Protect Sādhana**

To protect this morning sādhana, we must also plan our boundaries. Planning ourselves means we don’t sit around with people who have no sādhana.

If you are a devotee, you cannot sit around with non-devotees past midnight. They have no morning sādhana, so they are free to sit up late, drink coffee or tea, and talk about nonsense. They will discuss worldly politics, who is getting married, who has children going to school, and who has died. 

What is the benefit of this for you? Nothing! 

If you sit with them, they will be happy, but you will not be happy. Tomorrow morning when your alarm rings for Brahma Muhūrta, your body will demand sleep, your mind will make excuses, and your sādhana will suffer.

Therefore, you must plan your boundaries. You must have the courage to stand up after a few minutes and say: *“I am very sorry. I have to go and sleep. Tomorrow I have many spiritual duties to complete before I go to work.”*

---

## **But What About Emergencies?**

Someone may object: "Life is too dynamic. I make my plan, but something else always comes up—an office call, an urgent service—and the plan falls apart." That does happen sometimes, but not every day. When it happens, that is called an emergency, and of course we adjust. But we should still plan, and work toward the plan, because a careful plan is exactly what lets us absorb an emergency without it destroying our sādhana.

Think of a Third World country with heavy traffic. If your journey takes an hour, you don't leave exactly one hour before you need to arrive—you leave an hour and a half early. Then if there is a delay, you simply absorb it. If you plan like God—arriving with zero margin—only God can succeed at that, because only God does not need margin. We are not God. So we plan with margin, and then even the emergencies that come cannot touch our sādhana.

Plan your day, establish your boundaries, and follow them strictly. That is how you will control your mind, protect your sādhana, and achieve success.

## **Chapter 6: Heart Check**

### **Am I Properly Engaged?**

* **The Lamp and the Ghee:** When the mind is absorbed in sense gratification, it improperly burns and blackens our consciousness. When it is filled with ghee, it is brightly lit. Honestly examine your mind today: is it properly engaged in planning your services, reading, and hearing, or is it wandering off and blackening your day?
* **The Sādhana Vaccine:** What time do you complete your japa? Are you taking your "sādhana vaccine" early in the morning before exposing your mind to the office, social media, and non-devotee association?
* **Squeezing the Dead Body:** Identify one material desire where your mind is currently whispering: *"Squeeze it more! Squeeze it more, you will get some juice out of it!"* How will you practice detachment and refuse to listen to this false promise of the mind?
* **Protecting My Boundaries:** Look at your evening schedule. Are you sitting late into the night with people who have no sādhana, or scrolling on your phone, and allowing your early-morning rising to suffer? What specific boundary will you set tonight?
