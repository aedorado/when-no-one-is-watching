# Chapter 15: Cry Like a Queen

When the mortal body of our spiritual master expires, what should be our response? How should we, who have received his fatherly protection and guidance, navigate the devastating, heart-breaking pain of his physical disappearance?

We find our defining answer in the pages of the *Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam* (4.28.47), where the great sage Nārada Muni describes the daughter of King Vidarbha—the queen who was left alone in the forest after the departure of her husband, King Purañjana [1]:

> *ātmānaṁ śocatī dīnam*
> *abandhuṁ viklāvaṁ śuci*
> *aśru-mukhī kuca-yugmaṁ*
> *susvaraṁ paruroda sā*

**Translation by Śrīla Prabhupāda:** 
**“Being now alone and a widow in that forest, the daughter of Vidarbha began to lament, incessantly shedding tears, which shook her breasts, and crying very loudly.”**

Our supreme preceptor, Śrīla Prabhupāda, writes an extraordinarily moving, heavy instruction in his purport to this verse: 

**“Figuratively, the queen is supposed to be the disciple of the king. Thus, when the mortal body of the spiritual master expires, his disciples should cry exactly as a queen cries when the king leaves his body. However, the disciple and spiritual master are never separated because the spiritual master always keeps company with the disciple as long as the disciple follows strictly the instructions of the spiritual master. This is called the association of vāṇī. Physical presence is called vapu. As long as the spiritual master is physically present, the disciple should serve the physical body of the spiritual master. And when the spiritual master is no longer physically existing, the disciple should serve the instructions of the spiritual master [1].”**

The entire teaching of this chapter is already contained in that final sentence. Everything that follows is simply an unpacking of what it means to cry like the queen, and then to serve the instructions once the body is gone.

---

## **Tears of Separation vs. Morose Complacency**

We must cry. 

If we have genuine, deep feelings for the person who has saved our lives, how can we not weep? You cannot claim to have a spiritual heart-connection and not shed tears of grief when he is physically gone. If you have feelings, you cry because you feel the absence of the person. One who does not cry when the spiritual master physically departs simply has no connection with him. 

But we must understand exactly *why* we are crying. 

We are not crying because we think the spiritual master has died. No! A pure devotee never dies; he lives eternally in his words and instructions. We sing this truth every single morning: *cakṣu-dāna dilo yei, janme janme prabhu sei*—he who has given me spiritual eyes is my master birth after birth [3]. That relationship is eternal; it does not end with a body. Whether he gives us dīkṣā or śikṣā, the one who gives us prema is our eternal spiritual master, and that relationship is permanent—here, and in Goloka. In truth, our service to him now is not a passing duty; it is practice for what we will do eternally in the spiritual world. This life is the training ground for our eternal occupation with Kṛṣṇa and with the spiritual master, and that relationship has to be established right here.

That is how the non-devotees cry. Worldly people weep in *tama-guṇa*—the mode of ignorance—because they think the person is finished, the love has ended, and everything is destroyed. But for us, there is no such thing as death. The preaching and disciplic potency of a bona fide spiritual master do not end with the body; his mission continues for eternity.

We are crying simply because we miss his physical form—his *vapu*. We miss his physical association, his sweet, smiling face, and his fatherly guidance. We are crying out of intense, overflowing appreciation for what he has done for us. Sincere tears of appreciation are the natural, beautiful manifestation of our connection.

This is why the tears will only come if the relationship is real. One who has only an official or managerial relationship with the spiritual master—one who admires him the way one admires a hero, rather than actually worshipping him as Guru—will feel nothing when he disappears, because there was never a spiritual connection to lose. Hero worship is not the same as Guru-pūjā. A bona fide disciple's relationship is built through sincere, unmotivated service and through loyalty—through obedience. This is the same loyalty Mahārāja Pṛthu showed: he carried the instruction of his spiritual master on his very head [4]. When we accept initiation, the spiritual master gives us the holy name and, in that very act, lifts the load from our head; that is his duty toward us, and our duty in return is obedience for the rest of our lives.

But while the tears of separation are sweet and necessary, they must never be allowed to freeze into morose, paralyzed helplessness. Tears of appreciation are not the same as worldly lamentation. If we are just sitting in a corner, crying in morose sadness, thinking that everything is lost and there is no hope left—that is a sign of ignorance! 

Lamentation is in the mode of darkness. Being sad and morose is not *bhakti*; it is *tama-guṇa*. A disciple's crying should be a crying of gratitude, not of defeat. Our tears must cleanse our hearts, not paralyze our hands. The physical disappearance of the Guru is the time to prove what we have been learning from him when he was physically present.

---

## **The Arjuna and Duryodhana Test of Hearing**

Why do some disciples remain in ignorance after the Guru departs, while others make rapid progress? It all depends on how we hear. 

Let us look at the battlefield of Kurukṣetra [2]. Lord Kṛṣṇa stood in the middle, speaking the sublime philosophy of the *Bhagavad-gītā*. Arjuna was seated on his chariot on one side, and Duryodhana was present on the opposite side. 

Duryodhana did not hear a single word! He was physically there in the middle of the battlefield, but his heart was blocked by envy, pride, and material calculations. Therefore, he failed completely. But Arjuna heard every single word with deep, submissive attention, took the instructions on his head, and successfully executed his duty [2].

This is why hearing is everything. Sūta Gosvāmī himself offers his respectful obeisances to his own spiritual master, Śrī Śukadeva Gosvāmī, glorifying him as one who has the power to enter the heart of his audience—not physically, but through the sheer force of his speech, destroying the darkness of ignorance simply by being heard. That is the potency carried in the spoken word of a bona fide spiritual master [5].

Similarly, the spiritual master speaks the truth publicly to everyone. He speaks to the entire crowd. But only very few people actually hear. Those who hear with a sincere, open heart will practice and attain perfection, while those who treat hearing as a mechanical ritual will remain dry, empty, and unchanged.

---

## **Connecting to the Powerhouse of Vāṇī**

The spiritual master is the representative of Kṛṣṇa who carries and distributes His divine potency. But our absolute responsibility as disciples is to establish a genuine, heart-centered connection to his *vāṇī*—his instructions.

Imagine you build a house and get all the electrical fittings perfectly done. But if that house is not connected to the powerhouse, what will happen when you flip the switch? Nothing! The house will remain in darkness. The light is everywhere in the city, but it is not inside your house, because the connection to the powerhouse was never made.

One can be in this movement for years, even for lifetimes. You can wear the saffron dress, perform elaborate Deity worship, or manage big projects. But if you don't have a genuine, heart-centered connection with the spiritual master, you are on your own, and mental speculation will be your only guide. If that connection is never made, we will end up worshipping our own mind as our deity, not the spiritual master—and that will not last. Sincere, attentive hearing is what establishes the wire to the powerhouse of *vāṇī*.

---

## **“Maintenance is Māyā” and the Call to Expand**

The tears we shed must not be the end of our relationship with the Guru; they must become the beginning of our active responsibility. We cry, we appreciate, we remember his instructions, and then **we must move!** 

This is a movement—the Hare Kṛṣṇa movement! And **movement means we have to move. If we don’t move, Māyā will move us!** You cannot stay in one place. If you sit still in your grief, Māyā will quickly drag you down into complacency and dry laziness. 

**And remember: Maintenance is Māyā!** 

To simply keep the temple clean, maintain the existing ten disciples, and keep things static and “safe” is a failure! Just trying to keep things going as they are is Māyā! 

The physical disappearance of the Guru is the ultimate, urgent call to action. It is the time for us to take risks, to train future leaders, and to expand the mission to every town and village.

Some disciples, hearing that the Guru has disappeared, lament: *“Now there is no hope left. My life is finished.”* That is not the response of one who has actually heard. Those who have listened properly say instead: *“This is the time for me to act, to expand what my spiritual master has established.”* When the spiritual master departs, he does not carry away his spiritual potency with him—he leaves it to the disciples he has empowered to represent him. As long as the spiritual master is physically present, the disciple serves his physical body. When he is no longer physically present, the disciple serves his instructions. Let us rise up. Let us dry our tears of lamentation and replace them with the active fire of unmotivated service, and take the order of the spiritual master on our head.

## **Chapter 15: Heart Check**

### **The Powerhouse Connection**
Take a few quiet minutes to examine the reality of your connection:

1. **Vāṇī vs. Vapu:** Am I depending on the physical form of the Guru, or have I built an unshakeable connection to his instructions (*vāṇī*) that keeps me strong even in his physical absence?
2. **The Arjuna Test:** When I sit in class or read the scriptures, am I listening with the deep, submissive focus of Arjuna, or is my heart blocked by my own pride and material calculations like Duryodhana?
3. **Tears of Appreciation, Not Lamentation:** When I think of the Guru's physical absence, do my feelings move me toward gratitude and service, or do they settle into morose, paralyzed sadness?
4. **Maintenance is Māyā:** Am I sat comfortably in a corner, just trying to "maintain" my routine, or am I actively taking spiritual risks to expand the mission and please my spiritual master?
5. **The Test of Initiation:** Has there been a significant, honest change in my life since I was initiated—or am I, in substance, the same person I was before, simply wearing different clothes?
