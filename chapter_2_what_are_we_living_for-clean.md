# Chapter 2: What Are We Living For?

Have you ever stopped to think about what you are actually doing with this life? 

I know we are all very busy. We have so many plans, so many programs, and so many desires. But if you look closely, where is all this activity actually taking us? 

Almost all of our desires, all of our struggles, and all of our interests are centered on just one thing: this temporary bag of skin, bones, and stool that we call the body. We spend our mornings, our afternoons, and our nights trying to feed it nicely, dress it nicely, protect it nicely, and make it comfortable. 

But let me ask you a very honest question: *What are we living for?* 

Is it just to keep this physical body going for as long as possible? Is that the ultimate success of a human being? 

---

## The Man on the Crutches

Some time ago, I saw something that completely stopped me in my tracks and forced me to think deeply about this material world. 

I saw a man who was completely, severely deformed. By any ordinary human standard, his physical situation was tragic. He had no control over his legs, and his hands were so twisted that it was extremely difficult for him to use them. He sat on a pair of crutches, depending entirely on other people for every basic need—every meal, every bath, everything. There was no hope of medical treatment or physical recovery; he was going to be in this condition for the rest of his life.

As I was watching, this man began to cross a busy road. Suddenly, a motor vehicle came speeding down the street, heading straight toward him. 

At that moment, a shocking thing happened. The sheer speed and frantic energy that this deformed man used to drag his crutches and dodge that vehicle was unbelievable. He moved with an absolute, desperate intensity just to save himself from being hit. 

I watched him reach the other side of the road, and I began to contemplate: *What is the purpose of this person’s life?* 

Materially speaking, he has nothing. He has no independence, no physical comfort, and no worldly prospects. He suffers at every moment. So why was he fighting so desperately, with every ounce of his energy, just to keep that severely afflicted body alive for one more day?

The answer is both simple and profound: **the soul by nature is eternal**. 

Because the soul is eternal, it completely rejects death. No matter how miserable, broken, or 100% useless our material body becomes, we still desperately want to hold onto it. We do not want to die. And so, the entire struggle of the conditioned soul in this material world is reduced to a single, exhausting goal: *keeping this temporary physical shell alive for as long as possible*. 

---

## Better Than the Animals?

We think we are very advanced because we are human beings. We go to engineering colleges, earn degrees, and work hard every day, all just to feed and maintain this body. 

But if we look at our activities honestly, what are we doing that is so special? We eat, we sleep, we defend, and we mate. And if you look carefully, you will find that **every other species of life does these things far better than us**! 

An animal does not need to pay school fees or rent a house to stay. An animal does not need to take transport for its enjoyment. Nature has already provided everything it needs, without any struggle on its part. They have no anxiety about tomorrow, no fear of where their next meal will come from. Any aspect of sense gratification you can think of, the animals are executing perfectly, without any of our mental stress.

So if our life is only about eating nicely, sleeping nicely, and protecting our bodies, we are not living as human beings. We are just dressed-up animals. 

---

## Even the Greatest Are Frustrated

Even the greatest living entities in this material world are frustrated. Mārkaṇḍeya Ṛṣi lived a life that every living being desires—and still, he was not satisfied. Even Lord Brahmā, who lives the longest life possible in the material world, feels miserable, and eventually, his body also has to change. 

Karmīs are frustrated. Jñānīs are frustrated. Munis and yogīs are frustrated. Only the bhaktas are not frustrated—they are peaceful. Kṛṣṇadāsa Kavirāja Gosvāmī confirms this: *kṛṣṇa-bhakta niṣkāma, ateva śānta*—Kṛṣṇa's devotees are without material desire, and that is why they are peaceful. [1]

So why are we going to the spiritual master asking for material adjustments? 

Why do we go to a *spiritual* master and ask him to fix our business, improve our health, or make our material lives comfortable? 

This is like going to a multi-billionaire and begging him for a single piece of dry bread. Material adjustments are already available to every species of life, without any need for a Guru or a scripture. 

When we go to a spiritual master, we should ask for something that is **not of this material world**. We must ask for a permanent solution to our eternal problem. 

---

## The Formula of the Ācāryas

There is a beautiful verse in the twelfth canto of the *Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam* (12.10.34) where Mārkaṇḍeya Ṛṣi is standing before Lord Śiva. Lord Śiva is the greatest of the Vaiṣṇavas (*vaiṣṇavānāṁ yathā śambhuḥ*) [2], full of all perfection and capable of granting any material blessing in the universe. 

But listen to what Mārkaṇḍeya Ṛṣi begs of him:

> *varam ekaṁ vṛṇe ’thāpi pūrṇāt kāmābhivarṣaṇāt* 
> *bhagavaty acyutāṁ bhaktiṁ tat-pareṣu tathā tvayi* [3]

He prays: *\”I request only one blessing from you, who are full of all perfection and able to shower down the fulfillment of all desires. I ask to have unfailing, unalloyed devotion (Acyuta-bhakti) for the Supreme Personality of Godhead, and for his dedicated devotees—especially you.\”*

Mārkaṇḍeya knew that Lord Śiva could bless him with anything in this world, but he also knew that such material things are available anywhere, from anyone. He did not need to come to Śiva for that. He asked only for *bhakti*. 

The great ācārya, **Śrīla Bhaktivinoda Ṭhākura**, once answered a beautiful question: *“What is the beauty of Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam?”* Is it the print quality? Is it the design? Or is it even the philosophy?

He said: *“No. The true beauty of Śrīmad-Bhāgavatam is that it strictly forbids its followers from accessing or desiring anything from Kṛṣṇa except bhakti, pure devotional service.”* [4]

Why? Because *bhakti* is the only thing Kṛṣṇa is missing. He is the proprietor of everything, but He is conquered only by love. 

And *bhakti* is the only thing we actually need. 

Think about it: in every lifetime you have had in this material world, you have already had a mother, a father, a husband, a wife, and children. You have had homes, wealth, and education. Whether you were born as a human, a demigod, a cat, or an insect, you had a family and you had a body. 

But there are only two things you do not get in every birth: **a bona fide Guru and Lord Kṛṣṇa**. 

These are the greatest treasures—the two things not given to us in every birth. If you find a genuine spiritual master, and through him you find Kṛṣṇa, your material existence is terminated for eternity. You go back home, back to Godhead. 

---

## Sādhus are Not Business Masters

Today, we have turned spiritual life into a supermarket. 

People go to sādhus and gurus to ask for material blessings, physical health, or business advice. But why go to a sādhu for business advice? If you want to be a successful businessperson, go to an experienced businessman—he will train you much better! If you are sick, go to a hospital and pay a doctor. These days, we even have so many *asādhus*—false holy men who exploit this very desire, telling people, "Give me some money, and I will give you good health." But think about it: if he can give you good health, how long will he himself remain in that body? A charlatan's blessing cannot even save himself from old age and death—so what can it really do for you?

A spiritual master is not a business master or a medical doctor. His profession is **Kṛṣṇa**. 

All of Śrīla Prabhupāda's godbrothers gave their own centers different names. He named his differently too: the International Society for Krishna Consciousness. What does that mean? It means we do not ask for anything except Kṛṣṇa consciousness in this movement. If we ask for something else, we will not get it—and if we somehow do get it, we will only end up frustrated, because this society was never meant for anything else.

I remember, some years ago, I was invited to speak at a school. The principal told me beforehand, "Don't talk about God here." I said, "Then what should I speak on? Mathematics? I am not a mathematician. You already have teachers for physics, for chemistry, for every subject in this school. Do you have a teacher for Kṛṣṇa? No. So that is the only thing I have to offer. If you don't want me to speak on that, then I have nothing to say here." And I left.

Śrīla Bhaktivinoda Ṭhākura beautifully wrote: *\”O Sādhu! Your business is to distribute Kṛṣṇa. I do not have Kṛṣṇa, and that is why I have come to beg at your feet.\”* [5]

Dhruva Mahārāja gives us the same lesson from another angle. When Kuvera offered him a benediction, he did not ask for a kingdom or a comfortable life. He asked for only one thing: *“Let me never forget Kṛṣṇa, even for a single moment. Give me constant remembrance of Kṛṣṇa.”* [6]

The real business of a sādhu is to destroy our ignorance and cut our material attachments, planting the seed of Kṛṣṇa in our hearts.

---

## Are We Ready to Receive It?

But a spiritual master will not be physically present forever. So what do we do when he is no longer there to guide us? That is when we find out who is truly a disciple—our real behavior only shows itself once he is not there to see it. As Śrīla Prabhupāda said, everything we are doing will be tested—our service, our hearing, everything—and that examination comes at the time of death.[7]

This is why we must honestly ask ourselves: *Are we actually ready to receive what he is offering?* It is easy to look like a good disciple while he is watching. It is much harder to keep asking that same honest question when no one is watching at all. An intelligent person, doing the same thing every day with no real gain to show for it, should think twice before continuing. If something is wrong with what I am doing, I have to change it. That is what Kṛṣṇa consciousness actually asks of us.

There is another way we fool ourselves about this. Often, when we face difficulties, we do not actually solve them—we just \”shift the load\”. If our head hurts from carrying a heavy burden, we shift it to our shoulder and think we are making progress. But the load is still on our body! We are still in the material world. This is exactly what we do spiritually when we keep patching the body's problems instead of treating the soul's. Instead of trying to make temporary adjustments, we must change our pattern of thinking. We must rise above the bodily platform altogether. 

This is what it actually means to receive what a sādhu offers: if we have a healthy spiritual life, we will have a healthy material life; if our spiritual life is sick, our material life will be sick too—because everything depends on the spiritual, whether we realize it or not. Every human being should go to a sādhu for exactly this purpose. That, after all, is the very reason we have been given this body. It is not for anything else.

---

## Method and Intention

We do not need to invent new, artificial processes. We are not going to change the morning temple program from 4:30 AM to 6:30 AM. We are not going to change the Hare Kṛṣṇa Maha-mantra, and we are not going to change our scriptures or our Deities. We are going to do this same practice for the rest of our lives. 

But we must examine **how** we are doing it. What is our method? What is our internal intention?

Sincerity does not stay hidden. If you eat nicely, it shows in your body; if you eat poorly, that shows too. In the same way, if our real intention is to please Kṛṣṇa and Guru, it will show. We cannot hide our true motivation forever.

But even this is only the beginning. What is the actual beginning of spiritual life? No illicit sex, no gambling, no intoxication, no meat-eating, chanting our sixteen rounds every day. Have we even transcended that much? Some of us have been practicing for fifty years and are still struggling at this same beginning level. Many of us are deceiving ourselves, thinking, "I chant my sixteen rounds every day, so I will go back to Godhead." Will we really go back to Godhead like that? Do we even understand the language spoken there?

When we chant our rounds every day, what is the quality of our focus?

And most importantly: *How do we think about our fellow travelers on this path?* When another devotee is chanting or serving just as we are, are our thoughts about them sweet, or are they nasty?

This is accountability. It is not enough to say, "I get up every day, take a shower, and do some service—that is fine." It is not fine. Fine means: am I actually doing what I am supposed to be doing? We have to regularly stop and ask ourselves that question.
